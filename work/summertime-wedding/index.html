Summertime Wedding

Most wedding films are a music video. This one is a documentary.

The idea

A highlights film compresses a wedding into a few minutes and a song. It’s great for sharing and it’s what most couples want. But it throws away the best material: the offhand line from a dad during getting-ready, the officiant’s joke that only made sense if you knew them, the toast that went off script.

The Documentary package exists for couples who want that material kept. This film is the clearest example of why.

Location: Michigan. No time limit, no associate shooters. One videographer, two cameras, one drone.

Recording for sound bites

If the film is built on sound bites, the shoot has to be built on capturing them.

  • Lav mics on the groom and officiant for the ceremony, plus a recorder on the sound board for toasts and the DJ.
  • On-camera shotgun as a safety net everywhere else.

I shoot for the ear as much as the eye. A tight shot of someone laughing is worth nothing without the line that caused it.

The structure

The film runs in the order the day happened. No flash-forwards, no teaser at the top. A documentary earns its ending; it doesn’t preview it.

Each section opens with a sound bite, and the visuals follow the words. Getting ready. First look. Ceremony. Cocktail hour. Toasts. Dancing. The vows and toasts play at full length, because cutting them for time defeats the purpose of the package.

Music is under the dialogue, never on top of it. When the room goes quiet in the film, it’s because the room went quiet that day.

The cut

Edited in DaVinci Resolve.

The edit for a film like this is mostly listening. I log every usable line first, before touching a single shot. Then I build a paper cut from the audio, then lay picture on top. It is slower than cutting to music. It is also the only way a wedding film ends up being about the couple instead of about the song.
